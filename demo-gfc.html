DEMO_08 // FINANCE

How the 2008 financial crisis actually worked

You watched The Big Short. You saw people get rich betting against housing. This page is about everything underneath that: how a handful of ordinary home loans turned into a collapse that shook the world, and what it did to real families.

No finance background needed, just common sense. We'll follow one mortgage from a family's kitchen table through Wall Street, up through layer after layer of financial engineering, and back down into the real world when it all fell apart. At each stop there are interactive widgets (move the sliders, they're the point) and a few practice questions.

The whole story in one picture (click any step)

Seven steps. Each one made the risk look smaller. None of them made it smaller. At the end of the page you'll see this same picture again, with what broke at each step.

The 60-second version

  1. Lenders made loans to almost anyone, because they planned to sell the loans immediately instead of being repaid themselves.
  2. Wall Street bought the loans by the thousand, bundled them into bonds, and sold slices of those bonds to investors worldwide.
  3. Rating agencies called most of those slices "AAA" (as safe as US government debt), partly because their models assumed home prices wouldn't fall everywhere at once.
  4. The leftover risky slices were bundled again into new bonds called CDOs, and again most of the new pile was stamped AAA.
  5. Banks bought huge amounts of these "safe" bonds with borrowed money, about $30 borrowed for every $1 of their own, and insurers like AIG sold protection on them without setting aside money for a payout.
  6. Then house prices stopped rising. Borrowers who had counted on refinancing couldn't. Defaults rose, the bottom slices were wiped out, then "AAA" slices were hit far harder than anyone's models said was possible.
  7. Because banks had so little cushion, small losses made them insolvent, nobody knew who held the bad assets, and lending froze. Layoffs and foreclosures made house prices fall further. Around and around.

Warm-up

01

Mortgages 101

Everything starts with a family buying a house.

want to buy a $300,000 house. They don't have $300,000, nobody does, so they get a mortgage: a loan from a bank that is secured by the house itself. If they stop paying, the bank can take the house and sell it. This is called foreclosure, and the house is the collateral.

  • Principal: the amount borrowed. Interest: the lender's fee for lending, charged as a yearly percentage of what you still owe.
  • Down payment: the part you pay up front from your own savings. 20% is the traditional amount; Mia put down 5%.
  • Equity = what the house is worth − what you still owe. It's the part you really own. Underwater means that number is negative.
  • Everything is paid back in equal monthly payments over, typically, 30 years.
TRY IT The mortgage calculator

One thing to notice: in the first years, most of your payment is interest. You own very little of your house, because you've paid off very little. That makes a small fall in prices dangerous, because it can erase your tiny equity.

Prime, subprime, Alt-A, and the weird products

Banks sort borrowers by creditworthiness:

And the products that made it worse: 2/28 ARMs (a cheap fixed "teaser" rate for 2 years, then an adjustable rate for 28 years), interest-only loans, option ARMs (you could pay less than the interest and watch your balance grow), "piggyback" 80/20 loans (borrow the down payment too), and NINJA loans (No Income, No Job or Assets).

02

Lenders stop caring

How "making a loan" turned into "making a product to sell."

For most of the 20th century a bank made a loan and kept it. If Mia stopped paying, the bank lost money, so it checked her income and the house's value carefully. Economists call this originate-to-hold.

By the 2000s the model had flipped. A lender like made a loan, collected a fee, and sold the loan within days or weeks to a Wall Street bank such as . That's originate-to-distribute.

Who bears the risk?

Selling loans wasn't new: had bought "conforming" (safe, well-documented) mortgages for decades, guaranteeing them against default. What changed was a flood of private buyers, Wall Street, who wanted millions of higher-yielding loans to package and sell. Subprime lending exploded to roughly $600 billion a year by 2005–06, about one new mortgage in five.

Everyone along the chain was paid up front:

  • Mortgage brokers earned commissions per loan, and often more for steering you into a higher-rate loan (a "yield spread premium"), even if you'd have qualified for a cheaper one.
  • Appraisers who didn't bring in the value the lender wanted didn't get hired again.
  • Lenders got fees plus a profit when they sold the loan. They needed volume to feed Wall Street's appetite.
REAL LIFE

Countrywide and "the most dangerous product in existence"

Countrywide, led by Angelo Mozilo, was the largest US mortgage lender, with roughly one of every six new mortgages in 2006. In a 2006 email later quoted by the SEC, Mozilo described one of the company's subprime "80/20" products as "the most dangerous product in existence". Countrywide kept selling such loans. It was nearly insolvent by August 2007 and was sold to Bank of America in 2008. Mozilo later settled SEC fraud charges for $67.5 million without admitting wrongdoing.

03

Pooling loans into bonds (MBS)

Securitization: turning thousands of mortgages into one tradeable thing.

Wall Street banks like buy thousands of loans from lenders like and put them in a pile. That pile is placed inside a legal shell, a trust (the ). The trust then sells bonds whose payments come from the pile. These are called mortgage-backed securities (MBS), and the process is securitization.

A bond is just a loan that investors make to someone: they hand over cash now and get paid interest and principal back over time. Here the "borrower" is the pile of homeowners.

How an MBS is built and paid

Thousands of Mias (like Mia: $1,993/month) send payments to a , a company that just collects the checks, which forwards the cash to the trust, which pays whoever owns the bonds.

Why everyone loved it

  • Lenders got their cash back immediately and could make more loans.
  • Wall Street earned fees on every step.
  • Investors (pension funds, foreign banks, insurers) wanted something that paid more than US Treasuries but felt safe. Mortgages are backed by houses, and houses are boring. Global savings were piling up and needed a home.

And the core idea is sound! Pooling reduces risk when the loans are independent. If one borrower in 20 defaults, you can't predict which one, but you can predict that about 5% will. See it for yourself:

TRY IT Why pooling works (the law of large numbers)

Agency vs. private-label

MBS made by Fannie/Freddie ("agency") come with a guarantee: if homeowners default, Fannie or Freddie pays the bondholders. MBS made by Lehman, Bear, Countrywide and others ("private-label") have no guarantee. Those private-label deals funded most subprime and Alt-A loans, so they're where the disaster started.

04

Tranches: slicing the risk

Different bonds for different appetites, and who stamped them "safe."

Some investors, like a pension fund, want safe and don't mind a low return. Others, like hedge funds, want high returns and will take risk. So instead of selling identical bonds, the trust cuts the pile's cash flows into slices called tranches (French for "slice") with different priority.

Picture a building that floods from the basement. Whenever homeowners fail to pay, the "water" rises from the bottom:

  • Equity tranche (basement, ~5%): absorbs the first losses. Highest possible return, highest risk.
  • BBB tranche and Mezzanine (AA/A) tranches: the middle floors.
  • Senior / AAA tranche (penthouse, ~80%): hit only after everything below is wiped out. Paid first, loses last, and pays the lowest interest.

Payments run the other way: money flows down from the top, so the AAA investors are paid before anyone else. (Illustrative sizes and yields; real deals varied.)

The referees: rating agencies

Nobody can read a 600-page prospectus covering 5,000 loans. So investors relied on letter grades from and Standard & Poor's: AAA (as safe as it gets), then AA, A, BBB... down through "junk". Many pension funds and banks were only allowed to hold AAA or investment-grade bonds.

Three problems:

  1. Conflict of interest. The banks creating the bonds paid the agencies. If one agency was tough, the bank could shop around. In 2006 Moody's rated nearly 30 mortgage-related securities every working day. Over 2000–2007 it gave AAA to about 45,000 of them, while only six private US companies carried a AAA rating in 2010.
  2. Thin history. The agencies' models were fed historical data in which US house prices had not fallen nationwide since the 1930s, and subprime had barely existed before the late 1990s.
  3. A hidden assumption about correlation (below).

The assumption that broke everything

Earlier you saw pooling work because each loan was an independent coin flip. In reality, if house prices fall everywhere, many loans fail for the same reason, which statisticians call correlation (see your Correlation demo). Same average default rate, very different tails:

05

CDOs: bonds made of bonds

Where Mr. Chau comes in, and where the leverage inside the product is created.

A strange problem arose. Big investors bought the AAA slices, thrill-seekers took the equity, but the BBB slices were hard to sell: too risky for a pension fund, too boring for a hedge fund. Wall Street had thousands of them left over, and as long as they couldn't sell them, the banks couldn't buy more mortgages.

The solution was creative: do it again. Gather the BBB slices of many different mortgage deals into one new pile, put that in a new shell, and slice that into tranches. The new product is a collateralized debt obligation (CDO). Because the new pile held slices from 100 different deals, the rating agencies' models said it was well diversified, and about 75% of the CDO was stamped AAA, even though every ingredient was a BBB.

Building a CDO
REAL LIFE

Wing Chau and the CDO manager

In The Big Short, Steve Eisman's team meets Wing Chau, who ran Harding Advisory and managed CDOs for . A CDO manager picks which BBB slices go in. Chau's firm earned fees tied to the size of the CDOs it managed, and Merrill earned fees for creating and selling them. Everyone in the chain was paid for finishing deals. Nobody was paid to ask whether the underlying mortgages were any good. The film's Las Vegas conference scene is a famous dramatization of how thin the checking was in the middle of the chain.

Merrill was one of the largest CDO underwriters of 2006–07 and later wrote off tens of billions on them. It was sold to Bank of America on Sept 14, 2008.

Where the leverage comes from

"Leverage" means a small change in one thing produces a big change in another. A CDO is built-in leverage: since it contains only the thin BBB layers, a modest loss on the underlying mortgages wipes out most of those layers, and that loss lands on the CDO. A CDO's AAA slice is protected by its own cushion, but the cushion is made of the very thing that's being destroyed. Slide the mortgage loss up and watch:

Wait, was this just a mistake?

Partly. But three things kept the machine running: (1) correlation was ignored, so the CDO "diversification" was fake; (2) fees were earned up front, so growth paid more than quality; (3) when real BBB slices ran out, banks created "synthetic" CDOs from insurance contracts (credit default swaps) on BBB slices that referenced mortgages without needing new loans, so it kept growing even after Wall Street ran out of real mortgages to package. It meant one mortgage's risk could be bet on again and again.

06

Insurance nobody could pay: AIG

Credit default swaps, and the company that sold them without a safety net.

Banks holding CDO slices wanted protection. A credit default swap (CDS) is like an insurance policy on a bond: you pay a small premium, and the seller promises to pay you if the bond loses value. (Unlike real insurance, you don't need to own the thing: anyone could buy a CDS, which is the bet that Burry, Eisman and others made in the film.)

was a natural seller. It had a top credit rating, tons of cash, and a London-based unit, AIG Financial Products (run by Joseph Cassano), that wrote CDS on the "super senior" AAA slices of CDOs, ones that its models said had essentially zero chance of losing money. It sold protection on roughly $78 billion of subprime-related CDOs, part of a CDS book of around $500 billion, and collected a premium of a small fraction of 1% a year. It also set aside very little for payouts, because the models said payouts would never happen.

How a credit default swap works
TRY IT Collect pennies, owe millions

When the CDOs lost value, AIG owed billions, and its own contracts said that if its credit rating was cut or the bonds fell in value, it had to hand over cash (collateral) immediately. On Sept 16, 2008, the day after Lehman failed and AIG's rating was cut, the Fed lent AIG $85 billion and took roughly 80% of the company. Over time the government's commitment reached about $182 billion. Much of that cash flowed straight through AIG to its trading partners, including Goldman Sachs, Société Générale, Merrill Lynch and Deutsche Bank.

Why save it? Because AIG was a hub: dozens of banks were relying on the same insurer. If it had failed, their "hedged" positions would have turned into naked losses overnight.

07

Borrowed money: leverage and repo

Why a few percent of losses was enough to kill a bank.

Leverage means using borrowed money to buy more than you could with your own. Buy a $100 house with $3 of your own savings and $97 borrowed ("33 to 1") and a mere 3% price rise doubles your money. But a 3% fall wipes you out.

Wall Street investment banks did exactly this with mortgage bonds. At the end of 2007, had around 33 dollars of assets for each dollar of its own capital, and about 31. A typical commercial bank was closer to 10. It looked brilliant: if your assets rise 2%, you earn 60% on your capital.

TRY IT A bank's balance sheet

Repo: borrowing against bonds, overnight

Where does a bank get $30 to borrow for every $1 it owns? A large chunk came from the repo market ("repurchase agreement"). The bank hands over bonds as collateral, receives cash, and promises to buy the bonds back tomorrow. It's like a pawn shop that you visit every single morning. Most repo loans were overnight. So every day, lenders had to choose to renew.

Overnight repo

1. Normal times

Lender sees the bonds are "AAA." Rolls the loan every morning. Nobody thinks about it.

2. Worry

Bond prices wobble. Lender asks for a bigger cushion (a "haircut"). The bank needs more cash than it has.

3. Run

Lender refuses to renew. The bank must sell bonds now into a falling market, pushing prices lower, so the next lender gets nervous...

It's a bank run, on a bank that has no depositors

This is "shadow banking": bank-like funding without bank-like protection (no FDIC insurance, no lender of last resort until the Fed stepped in). Even a bank worth more than it owes can die if funding vanishes.

REAL LIFE

Lehman's "Repo 105"

As fear grew, Lehman wanted its leverage to look lower at quarter-end. A later court examiner found it used an accounting maneuver ("Repo 105") to temporarily move roughly $50 billion of assets off its balance sheet each quarter-end in 2008, then bring them back after the reporting date. Investors were not told.

08

The crack: 2006 and 2007

Why the machine stopped, and why it took 18 months to become a crisis.

Why had prices been rising? A mix: cheap money after the cut rates to 1% in 2003, a flood of global savings, lax lending, and a self-fulfilling belief that "housing never falls." Speculators bought homes to flip them. The more lending expanded, the higher prices went. Higher prices made the loans look safer, so more lending followed.

But the machine needed constant fuel: new loans, so it could make new bonds. As qualified borrowers ran out, lenders chased riskier ones. The last loans made in 2006–07 were the worst: a growing share of borrowers missed their very first payment. And once the Fed had raised rates from 1% back to 5.25%, adjustable-rate loans began resetting, just as the "just refinance" escape plan stopped working.

Why was Bear Stearns rescued but not Lehman?

Still debated. Whatever the reason, the panic that followed (money funds, commercial paper, global interbank lending) is often traced to that weekend. The "bank fear" gauge, the LIBOR–OIS spread, normally about 0.1%, hit about 3.6% in October 2008. When banks won't lend to each other, businesses can't make payroll.

The ways it reached everyone

  • Neighborhood spillovers. A foreclosed, empty house lowers the value of every house nearby, including those of neighbors who never missed a payment. Las Vegas, Phoenix, inland California, Florida and Detroit saw prices fall by half or more.
  • Jobs. Construction collapsed first, then everything else as credit froze. Companies that rely on short-term borrowing for payroll and inventory couldn't borrow.
  • Retirement savings. The stock market's fall of about 57% shrank 401(k)s and pensions right before many people planned to retire.
  • Credit. Banks tightened lending on car loans, small-business credit lines, student loans, credit cards, and mortgages for years.
  • The world. European banks had bought huge amounts of US mortgage bonds. Britain's Northern Rock suffered a bank run in September 2007. Iceland's banks collapsed in 2008, and the shockwave contributed to Europe's debt crisis in the following years.

The response, and why many people were angry

The government acted at scale: the cut interest rates to near zero and bought trillions in bonds; authorized TARP ($700 billion, later cut to $475B; most of it was eventually repaid, with the government recovering more than it disbursed overall); Congress passed an $800B-ish stimulus in 2009. Bank rescues were fast. Help for homeowners was slow and small: the main program, HAMP, aimed at 3–4 million loan modifications and delivered roughly 1.7 million permanent ones.

Accountability was thin. Few top executives of the big banks were charged personally; the banks paid settlements instead (e.g., JPMorgan $13 billion in 2013, Bank of America $16.65 billion in 2014).

New rules followed: Dodd-Frank (2010) created the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, forced standard derivatives onto exchanges, set rules for winding down failing giants, and requires securitizers to keep ~5% of the risk ("skin in the game"). Basel III raised bank capital requirements, and the Fed began regular "stress tests."

Putting it all together

The same seven-step picture, with what broke at every step.

Where each link failed

Three ideas that explain the whole thing

  1. Risk was cut up and hidden, not removed. Pooling, tranching, ratings and insurance made the risk look small at every step. But the total risk (one huge bet that US house prices wouldn't fall) was unchanged, and it sat concentrated in a few giant, overextended firms.
  2. Incentives were upside-down. Brokers, lenders, banks, CDO managers and rating agencies were all paid for volume at the moment of sale, and the risk went to somebody else.
  3. Leverage and short-term funding made it fragile. With ~30 to 1 borrowing and funding that had to be renewed every night, a few percent of losses and a loss of confidence were enough.

Myth vs. reality

MYTH

"It was all irresponsible borrowers."

REALITY Many borrowers were steered into loans they couldn't afford, often by brokers paid for it; plenty who qualified for prime loans got subprime ones. Borrowers made choices, but the system was designed so that the people with the most information (lenders, banks, raters) bore the least risk.

MYTH

"Fannie and Freddie caused it."

REALITY Debated. They did buy risky mortgage bonds and loosened standards late, and they collapsed. But most of the riskiest loans (subprime, Alt-A) were packaged by private Wall Street firms with no government guarantee. The majority view of the official Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission was that they contributed but weren't the primary cause.

MYTH

"Nobody could have seen it coming."

REALITY A few investors did (the people in The Big Short), and many analysts warned of a housing bubble. The models failed partly because the incentives to question them were weak.

MYTH

"The bailouts were gifts to the banks."

REALITY It's both. Most direct TARP money was eventually repaid, and without it the panic might have ended in a second Great Depression. But the lopsided treatment, banks rescued quickly while homeowners and executives largely weren't held to account, is why trust in institutions fell.

Glossary: all the jargon in one place
Alt-A
Loans to borrowers with decent credit but undocumented income ("liar loans").
ARM (adjustable-rate mortgage)
A loan whose interest rate changes over time. A 2/28 has a fixed teaser for 2 years, then adjusts.
Collateral
An asset a lender can seize if you don't repay (the house, or bonds in a repo).
CDO
Collateralized debt obligation: a pile of bonds (often BBB slices of MBS) re-sliced into new tranches.
CDS (credit default swap)
An insurance-like contract that pays out if a bond loses value.
Correlation
How much things move together. High correlation = they fail together, so diversification stops working.
Equity (home)
House value − mortgage owed. (Equity tranche: the first-loss slice of a pool.)
Haircut
The cushion a repo lender demands: lending $95 against $100 of bonds is a 5% haircut.
Leverage
Using borrowed money to magnify gains and losses. 30:1 means $30 of assets per $1 of your own money.
MBS (mortgage-backed security)
A bond whose payments come from a pool of mortgages.
Originate-to-distribute
Making loans in order to sell them rather than hold them.
Repo
Short-term (often overnight) borrowing secured by bonds, renewed continually.
Securitization
Pooling loans and selling bonds backed by them.
Subprime
Loans to borrowers with weaker credit.
TARP
Troubled Asset Relief Program: the $700B (later $475B) bailout fund approved Oct 2008.
Tranche
A slice of a pool of loans with its own priority for being paid and for absorbing losses.
Underwater
Owing more on the house than it is worth.
